Starting with equation \ref{eq:radiusVsEccentric Anomaly} that gives the relationship between the distance from the focus and the ecentric anomaly
\begin{equation*}
r=A(1-e\cos E)
\end{equation*}
We can take the derivative with respect to time to get the relationship between the rate of change of distance with time and the ecentric anomally.
\begin{align}
\frac{dr}{dt} &= A \frac{d}{dt}\left(1-e\cos E \right)\nonumber \\
&= A \frac{dE}{dt}\frac{d}{dE}\left(1-e\cos E \right)\nonumber \\
&= eA \sin E \frac{dE}{dt} \label{eq:Kepler1}
\end{align}
Using equation \ref{eq:PolarElipse} for the equation of an elipse in polar form $r = \frac{A\left(1-e^2\right)}{ 1+e \cos \theta}$. We can invert this this to get

\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{r}= \frac{ 1+e \cos \theta}{A\left(1-e^2\right)} \\
\end{equation}
which will make taking the derivative with respect to time easier.
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{1}{r}\right) &= \frac{d}{dt}\left[ \frac{ 1+e \cos \theta}{A\left(1-e^2\right)}\right]\nonumber \\
\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{d}{dr}\left( \frac{1}{r}\right) &= \frac{1}{A(1-e^2)}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\frac{d}{d\theta}\left[  1+e \cos \theta \right]\nonumber \\
-\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{dr}{dt} &= \frac{-e \sin \theta}{A(1-e^2)}\frac{d\theta}{dt}
\end{align}
we can now substitute in equation \ref{eq:Kepler1} for $\frac{dr}{dt}$ to get
\begin{equation}
-\frac{1}{r^2} eA \sin E \frac{dE}{dt}= \frac{-e \sin \theta}{A(1-e^2)}\frac{d\theta}{dt} \label{eq:Kepler2}
\end{equation}
We can use equation \ref{eq:ElipseYTheta} and \ref{eq:ElipseYE} for y to obtain a relationship between $\sin \theta$ and $\sin E$
\begin{equation}
y = r \sin \theta = A\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E
\end{equation}
We can use this relationship to replace the $\sin \theta$ term in equation \ref{eq:Kepler2}
\begin{align}
-\frac{1}{r^2} eA \sin E \frac{dE}{dt}&= \frac{-e \sin \theta}{A(1-e^2)}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{-e A\sqrt{1-e^2}\sin E}{Ar(1-e^2)}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{-e \sin E}{r\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
\frac{-eA \sin E }{r^2} \frac{dE}{dt}&= \frac{-e \sin E}{r\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
\frac{dE}{dt}&= \frac{-r^2}{eA \sin E } \frac{-e \sin E}{r\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
\frac{dE}{dt} & = \frac{r}{A\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}
\end{align}
Finally we can replace $r$ with equation \ref{eq:radiusVsEccentric Anomaly} $r=A(1-e \cos E)$ to get
\begin{align}
\frac{dE}{dt} & = \frac{r}{A\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\\
 & = \frac{A(1-e\cos E)}{A\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\\
  & = \frac{(1-e\cos E)}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\label{eq:Kepler3}
\end{align}
Now we have an equation that links the rate of change of $E$ to the rate of change of $\theta$ in terms of just $E$ and $\theta$

\paragraph{Using Angular Momentum} given by equation \ref{eq:AngularMomentumScalar}
\begin{equation*}
l = m r^2 \frac{d\theta}{dt}
\end{equation*}
useing equation \ref{eq:radiusVsEccentric Anomaly} and rearranging we obtain
\begin{align}
l &= m r^2 \frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
\frac{d\theta}{dt}&= \frac{l}{m r^2}\nonumber  \\
\frac{d\theta}{dt}&= \frac{l}{m A^2\left(1-e \cos E \right)^2}
\end{align}
which we can now substitute in for $\frac{d\theta}{dt}$ term in equation \ref{eq:Kepler3}
\begin{align}
\frac{dE}{dt} & = \frac{(1-e\cos E)}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{d\theta}{dt}\nonumber \\
& = \frac{(1-e\cos E)}{\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{l}{m A^2\left(1-e \cos E \right)^2}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{l}{m A^2\sqrt{1-e^2}}\frac{1}{1-e \cos E}\nonumber \\
\left(1-e \cos E \right)\frac{dE}{dt}&= \frac{l}{m A^2\sqrt{1-e^2}}
\end{align}
Now you can see that the right hand side is just a constant, which is defined by properties of the orbit. Namely the angular momentum, the mass of the orbiting object, the semi major axis and the eccentricity. An interesting thing to note is that the term $A^2\sqrt{1-e^2}$ can be rewritten as the product $AB$ instead, so linking the major and minor axes. We will however keep it in it's original form for now. Since the right hand side is a constant we will simplify by choosing the constant $k$ for it. This constant $k$ is the \empth{Mean Motion}, later\footnote{see \ref{}} we will see that it's connected to the period of the orbit, and ends up being the mean angular velocity of the orbiting body.
\begin{equation}
k = \frac{l}{m A^2\sqrt{1-e^2}}\label{eq:Kepler4}
\end{equation}
so finally all that is left to do is to integrate our equation with respect to time.
\begin{align}
\int_{t_0}^t k dt &= \int_{t=t_0}^t\left(1-e \cos E \right)\frac{dE}{dt} dt\nonumber \\
k (t-t_0) &=\int_{E=0}^E\left(1-e \cos E \right)dE \nonumber \\
&=\int_0^E 1 dE - e \int_0^E \cos E dE\nonumber \\
&=E - e \sin E\\
\end{align}
Note we have chosen $t_0$ to be the point in time when $E=0$, which is the same as the statement of Kepler's equation.
\begin{equation}
E - e \sin E=k(t-t_0)\label{eq:KeplersEquation}
\end{equation}
