\section{Introduction}

Now that we have looked at central forces more generally, we can apply what we have found to gravitational attraction between two bodies.

The force between two particles a distance $r$ apart with the central larger mass $M$ and the smaller mass $m$ is given by
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F} = -\frac{G M m}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}}
\end{equation}

We will introduce the standard gravitational parameter $\mu = GM$ here to simplify the equations, we will also use
the specific force, ie the force per unit mass, also known as the acceleration for a body with fixed mass.

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathbf{F}}{m} = -\frac{\mu}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \label{eq:gravity0}
\end{equation}

\section{Gravitational Potential Energy}

As seen earlier, the potential energy is given by equation \refpage{eq:central-potentialEnergy}

\begin{align}
V(r)&=-\int_{\infty}^{r} -\frac{\mu m}{r'^2} dr' \nonumber \\
\frac{V(r)}{m}&=\mu \int_{\infty}^{r} \frac{1}{r'^2} dr' \nonumber \\
&=-\mu \left[ \frac{1}{r} - \frac{1}{\infty} \right] \nonumber \\
&= - \frac{\mu}{r}\label{eq:gravity1}
\end{align}

\section{Total Energy}

The specific total energy is then given by
\begin{equation}
\epsilon = \frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r} \label{eq:gravity7}
\end{equation}

\section{The Eccentricity Vector}

For inverse square central forces there is another constant of motion.
This is known as the eccentricity vector. The reason it is called this will
become clear later, but for this section we will work through it's derivation.
There are a lot of moving parts with this derivation so we will start with some
smaller seemingly independent calculations that will ultimately get us to the 
eccentricity vector and the fact it is a constant of the motion.

\subsection{Rate of change of velocity}

The rate of change of velocity is given by equation \refpage{eq:gravity0},
since the rate of change of velocity is the acceleration, which is just the
specific force, ie $\frac{\mathbf{F}}{m}$, so

\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf{v}}&=\frac{\mathbf{F}}{m}\nonumber \\
&= -\frac{\mu}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf{r}} \nonumber \\
&= -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r} \label{eq:gravity2}
\end{align}

\subsection{Rate of change of $\mathbf{K}$}

We will firstly define a vector which we will label $\mathbf{K}$ which is the vector product of 
the velocity vector and the specific angular momentum vector. The vector $\mathbf{K}$ doesn't have
any special significance by itself, we're introducing it now for book keeping purposes here to help
keep track of the steps in the derivation.

We will now look how this quantity $\mathbf{K}$ changes with respect to time.

\begin{align}
\frac{d\mathbf{K}}{dt}&=\frac{d}{dt}\left( \mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h} \right)\nonumber \\
 &= \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} \times \mathbf{h} + \mathbf{v} \times \frac{d\mathbf{h}}{dt} \nonumber \\
\end{align}

since we have already shown that the rate of change of angular momentum is zero\footnote{equation \ref{eq:central6} on page \pageref{eq:central6}}
our above equation simplifies to
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{K}}{dt} = \dot{\mathbf{v}} \times \mathbf{h}
\end{equation}

We can now substitute in equation \refpage{eq:gravity2} for $\dot{\mathbf{v}}$, leaving us with

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{K}}{dt} = -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{h}
\end{equation}

Recall that $\mathbf{h} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{v}$ leading to

\begin{equation}
\frac{d\mathbf{K}}{dt} = -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{v}
\end{equation}

Next we can use the triple vector product expansion\footnote{equation \ref{eq:vec1} on page \pageref{eq:vec1}}
to expand $\mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{v}$

\begin{align}
\dot{\mathbf{K}} &= -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \left[ (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{r}-(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{r})\mathbf{v} \right] \nonumber \\
&= -\frac{\mu}{r^3} \left[ (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{r}-r^2 \mathbf{v} \right] \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\mu}{r^3} \left[ r^2 \mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{r} \right] \label{eq:gravity4}
\end{align}

\subsection{Rate of change of the unit position vector}

\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}} & = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r}\nonumber \\
&=\frac {\dot{\mathbf{r}}r-\mathbf{r}\dot{r}}{r^2} \nonumber \\
&=\frac {{\mathbf{v}}-\dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r} \label{eq:gravity3}
\end{align}

where we have used the quotient rule\footnote{equation \ref{eq:calc-qr} on page \pageref{eq:calc-qr}}

Let's look at the rate of change of the magnitude of the position vector for a moment

\begin{align}
\frac{dr}{dt} &= \frac{d}{dt}(\sqrt{r^2})\nonumber \\
&=\frac{d \sqrt{u}}{du}\frac{du}{dt}\nonumber \\
&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\frac{du}{dt}
\end{align}
where we have introduced $u=r^2$
\begin{align}
\dot{r}&= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}\frac{du}{dt} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r^2}}\frac{dr^2}{dt} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{1}{2r}\frac{d}{dt}(\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{r}) \nonumber \\
&=\frac{1}{2r}(\dot{\mathbf{r}}\cdot\mathbf{r} + \mathbf{r}\cdot\dot{\mathbf{r}})\nonumber \\
&=\frac{2 \mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{2r} \nonumber \\
&=\frac{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{r} \label{eq:gravity6}
\end{align}

we can now put this result back into equation \refpage{eq:gravity3}

\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}} &=\frac {{\mathbf{v}}-\dot{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r} \nonumber \\
 &=\frac {{\mathbf{v}}-\frac{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r} \nonumber \\
 &= \frac{r^2}{r^2} \frac {{\mathbf{v}}-\frac{\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{r}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{{r^2\mathbf{v}}-(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v})r\hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^3} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{{r^2\mathbf{v}}-(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{r}}{r^3}
\end{align}
If we now multiply through by $\mu$

\begin{equation}
\mu \frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \frac{\mu}{r^2}\left[{r^2\mathbf{v}}-(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{r}\right]
\end{equation}

Now if we compare to equation \refpage{eq:gravity4}

\begin{align}
\mu \frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}} &= \frac{\mu}{r^2}\left[{r^2\mathbf{v}}-(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{r}\right] \nonumber \\
\frac{d \mathbf{K}}{dt} &= \frac{\mu}{r^3} \left[ r^2 \mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{r} \right] \nonumber \\
\end{align}

As you can see both are equal so we can equate them

\begin{align}
\frac{d \mathbf{K}}{dt}  &= \mu \frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}}  \nonumber \\
\frac{d \mathbf{K}}{dt} - \mu \frac{d}{dt}\hat{\mathbf{r}} &= \mathbf{0}\nonumber \\
\frac{d}{dt}\left[\mathbf{K} - \mu \hat{\mathbf{r}}\right] &= 0
\end{align}

This shows that the vector $\mathbf{K} - \mu \hat{\mathbf{r}}$ is a constant.

The eccentricity vector is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{e} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} - \hat{\mathbf{r}} \label{eq:gravity5}
\end{equation}

Which is just the vector we had multiplied by $\frac{1}{\mu}$ and so is also a constant.
We will see later why this form in particular is choosen, for now it is enough to know that
it is a third constant of the motion.
