\section{Introduction}

In this chapter we will look at the connection between the geometry of
ellipses and the physical constants that we've shown previously.

\section{The Apses}

The apses are the closest and furthest point from the focus in the orbit. 
In other words they are the maximum and minimum radius. At the apses
\begin{equation}
\frac{d r}{dt}=\dot{r} = 0
\end{equation}
We can then use equation \refpage{eq:gravity6} to show that the velocity is
perpendicular to the radius at the apses.
\begin{align}
\dot{r} &= \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{r} \nonumber \\
0 &= \frac{\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{r} \nonumber \\
0 &= \mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{v}
\end{align}
since the scalar product is zero, the two vectors must be 
perpendicular\footnote{or zero, but we are looking at non-zero solutions in
particular}. 

\subsection{Angular Momentum}

If the radius and velocity are perpendicular at the apses, then the magnitude
of the specific angular momentum is
\begin{equation}
h=p v_p = a v_a
\end{equation} 
where $v_p$ is the velocity at the periapsis and $v_a$ is the velocity at apoapsis.

\subsection{Energy}

We can now look at the specific total energy again, firstly at periapsis
\begin{align}
\epsilon &= \frac{v_p^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{p} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{h^2}{2p^2}-\frac{\mu}{p} \label{eq:phys-geom-energy1}
\end{align} 
then at apoapsis
\begin{align}
\epsilon &= \frac{v_a^2}{2}-\frac{\mu}{a} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{h^2}{2a^2}-\frac{\mu}{a}
\end{align} 
Since total energy is a constant these must be equal
\begin{align}
\frac{h^2}{2p^2}-\frac{\mu}{p} &= \frac{h^2}{2a^2}-\frac{\mu}{a} \nonumber \\
\frac{h^2}{2p^2}-\frac{h^2}{2a^2} &= \frac{\mu}{p} -\frac{\mu}{a} \nonumber \\
\frac{a^2h^2}{2a^2p^2}-\frac{p^2h^2}{2a^2p^2} &= \frac{a\mu}{ap} -\frac{p\mu}{ap} \nonumber \\
\frac{a^2h^2-p^2h^2}{2a^2p^2} &= \frac{a\mu-p\mu}{ap} \nonumber \\
h^2 \frac{a^2-p^2}{2 ap} &= (a-p)\mu \nonumber \\
h^2 &= \frac{2ap(a-p)}{a^2-p^2}\mu \nonumber \\
 &= \frac{2ap(a-p)}{(a-p)(a+p)}\mu \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2ap}{a+p}\mu \label{eq:phys-geom1}
\end{align}

\subsection{Velocity}
We now have an equation\refpage{eq:phys-geom1} for the specific angular momentum based purely
on the central mass and the geometry of the orbit. We can use this to
calculate velocities at the periapsis
\begin{equation}
v_p = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{p}\sqrt{\frac{2ap}{a+p}}
\end{equation}
and for the apoapsis
\begin{equation}
v_a = \frac{h}{a} = \frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2ap}{a+p}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Semi-Major Axis}
We can feed this result back into equation \refpage{eq:phys-geom-energy1}
\begin{align}
\epsilon &= \frac{h^2}{2p^2}-\frac{\mu}{p} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2ap}{2p^2(a+p)}\mu-\frac{\mu}{p} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\mu}{p}\left(\frac{a}{a+p}-1 \right) \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\mu}{p}\left(\frac{a}{a+p}-\frac{a+p}{a+p} \right) \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\mu}{p}\left(\frac{a-a-p}{a+p}\right) \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\mu}{p}\left(\frac{-p}{a+p}\right) \nonumber \\
&= \frac{-\mu}{a+p}
\end{align}
Using equation \refpage{eq:geom3} we can replace the $a+p$ term
\begin{align}
\epsilon &= \frac{-\mu}{2A} \nonumber \\
A &= \frac{-\mu}{2\epsilon}\label{eq:phys-geom5}
\end{align}

\subsection{Eccentricity}

We will now use the results of equation \refpage{eq:phys-geom1}

\begin{equation*}
h^2= \frac{2ap}{a+p}\mu
\end{equation*}
as before we can use equation \refpage{eq:geom3} to relpace $a+p$, additionally
we can use equation \refpage{eq:geom5} to replace the $ap$ term.
\begin{align}
h^2 &= \frac{2A^2(1-e^2)}{2A}\mu \nonumber \\
&= A(1-e^2)\mu
\end{align}
now we can use equation \refpage{eq:phys-geom5} to replace $A$
\begin{align}
h^2 &= A(1-e^2)\mu \nonumber \\
&=\frac{-\mu}{2\epsilon}(1-e^2)\mu \nonumber \\
\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu}&=e^2-1 \nonumber \\
1+\frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu^2}&=e^2 \label{eq:phys-geom8}
\end{align}

We could now calculate $a$ and $p$ from the energy and angular momentum, by
first using those to calculate A and e, and then using equation \refpage{eq:geom6}.
We will not do this here, this is more a note to say it can be done. When we
look at using initial conditions to calculate various constants, we will return
to the calculation then.

\section{Eccentricity Vector}

Now we will return to the eccentricity vector given by equation \refpage{eq:gravity5}.
\begin{equation*}
\mathbf{e} = \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} - \hat{\mathbf{r}} 
\end{equation*}
In the chapter we introduced the eccentricity vector, we claimed that it's 
length was the same as the eccentricity, which is why it was called the
eccentricity vector. At the time we did not show this to be the case, but
now we have all the machinary needed to show it to be the case.

We will need one result that we haven't yet seen, that is the squared
magnitude of h, or at least in form we haven't seen yet

\begin{align}
h^2 &= \mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{h} \nonumber \\
&= \mathbf{h}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v})
\end{align}
We can use equation \refpage{eq:vec2} to get the result
\begin{align}
h^2 &= \mathbf{h}\cdot(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}) \nonumber \\
&= \mathbf{r}\cdot(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{h}) \label{eq:phys-geom7}
\end{align}
Not a very exciting result yet, but it will come into play now.
Lets now look at the thing we actually want to know, the magnitude of the
eccentricity vector.

\begin{align}
e^2 &= \mathbf{e} \cdot \mathbf{e} \nonumber \\
&= \left( \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} - \hat{\mathbf{r}} \right) \cdot \left( \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} - \hat{\mathbf{r}} \right) \nonumber \\
&= \left( \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} \right)^2 - 2 \frac{\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}}{\mu} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} + \hat{\mathbf{r}} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}|^2}{\mu^2} - 2 \frac{(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h})\cdot \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{\mu} + 1 \nonumber \\
&= \frac{|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}|^2}{\mu^2} - 2 \frac{(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h})\cdot \mathbf{r}}{\mu r} + 1
\end{align}
We can use our previous equation \refpage{eq:phys-geom7} now,
as well as the fact that $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{h}$ are perpendicular
by construction, since $\mathbf{h}=\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{v}$, so it is
perpendicular to both $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{v}$. This means 
$|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}|=vh$.
\begin{align}
e^2 &= \frac{|\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h}|^2}{\mu^2} - 2 \frac{(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{h})\cdot \mathbf{r}}{\mu r} + 1 \nonumber \\
&= \frac{v^2h^2}{\mu^2} - 2 \frac{h^2}{\mu r} + 1 \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2v^2h^2}{2\mu^2} - 2 \frac{h^2\mu}{\mu^2 r} + 1 \nonumber \\
&= \frac{2h^2}{\mu^2}\left(\frac{v^2}{2} - \frac{\mu}{r}\right) + 1 \nonumber \\
\end{align}
Notice how the part in the brackets is the same as equation \refpage{eq:gravity7},
this means we can replace the whole bracket with $\epsilon$, leading to the result.
\begin{equation*}
e^2= \frac{2\epsilon h^2}{\mu^2} + 1 \nonumber \\
\end{equation*}
Which is the exact same form as equation \refpage{eq:phys-geom8},
this shows that the length of the eccentricity vector is in fact
the same as the eccentricity.
