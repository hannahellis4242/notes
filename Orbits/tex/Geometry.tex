\section{The String Construction}

One method of drawing an ellipse is to take a string of fixed length pinned to the two focus points of the ellipse. We can use this method to derive the standard equations for an ellipse. We will first start by setting up the geometry.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=0.3\textheight]{./img/string-method.png} 
\caption{The string method of constructing an ellipse} \label{fig:string-method}
\end{figure}

We will work in a two dimensional Cartesian coordinate system with an ellipse centered at the origin and orientated so that it's semi-major axis is directed along the x-axis and it's semi-minor axis orientated with the y axis. The two focus points $F$ and $-F$ are located on the x-axis a distance $f$ away from the origin, so that $F=(f,0)$ and $-F=(-f,0)$. Let $P$ be an arbitrary point on the ellipse with coordinates $P=(X,y)$\footnote{We use $X$ rather than $x$ here to avoid confusion with a later definition of $x$ as a distance measured from the focus $F$}.
The ellipse has semi-major axis length $A$ and semi-minor axis length $B$,  so that $-A \leq X \leq A$ and $-B \leq y \leq B$. Let $r$ be the distance from $F$ to $P$ and $d$ be the distance from $-F$ to $P$ and let the sum of these be S, so that

\begin{equation}
S = r + d
\end{equation}

where
\begin{align}
r &= \sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2} \nonumber \\
d &= \sqrt{(X+f)^2+y^2}
\end{align}

\subsection{Special cases and useful results}

From the construction method, we know that the sum is fixed.  We can find it's exact value by considering $P$ when $y=0$ and $X=A$, so that 
\begin{align}
r &= \sqrt{(A-f)^2+0^2}\nonumber \\
&=  \sqrt{(A-f)^2}\nonumber \\
&=  A-f\nonumber \\
d &= \sqrt{(A+f)^2+0^2}\nonumber \\
&= \sqrt{(A+f)^2}\nonumber \\
&= A+f\nonumber \\
S &= r + d \nonumber \\
&= A - f + A + f\nonumber \\
S &= 2A \label{eq:geom-sum}
\end{align}

This shows that the string must be twice the length of the semi-major axis to work.

Another interesting result is obtained when we look at the point $P=(0,B)$

\begin{align}
r &=  \sqrt{(0-f)^2+B^2}\nonumber \\
&=  \sqrt{f^2+B^2}\nonumber \\
d &= \sqrt{(0+f)^2+B^2}\nonumber \\
&= \sqrt{(f^2+B^2)^2}\nonumber \\
S &=2  \sqrt{(f^2+B^2)^2}
\end{align}

since we now know that $S=2A$ we can subtitute for $S$
\begin{align}
S &=2  \sqrt{(f^2+B^2)^2} \nonumber \\
2A &=2  \sqrt{(f^2+B^2)^2} \nonumber \\
A &=\sqrt{(f^2+B^2)^2} \nonumber \\
A^2 &=f^2+B^2 \label{eq:geom1}
\end{align}

You are more likely to see this stated as $B=A\sqrt{1-e^2}$ where $e$ or the eccentricity is defined to be 

\begin{equation}
e=\frac{f}{A} \label{eq:geom-eccentricity}
\end{equation}

so that $f=eA$

\subsection{Equation of an ellipse}

Now we know the value of $S$ we can look for a general equation that links $X$ and $y$. Firstly we write out the sum in full

\begin{align}
S & = r + d \nonumber \\
2A &= \sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2} +  \sqrt{(X+f)^2+y^2} \nonumber \\
2A -\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}  &=\sqrt{(X+f)^2+y^2}
\end{align}
Now we square both sides to get rid of the first square root

\begin{align}
\left(2A -\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}\right)^2 &=(X+f)^2+y^2\nonumber \\
4A^2 -4A\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}+(X-f)^2+\textcolor{red}{\cancel{y^2}} &=(X+f)^2+\textcolor{red}{\cancel{y^2}}\nonumber \\
4A^2 -4A\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}+\textcolor{green}{\cancel{X^2}} \textcolor{red}{- 2Xf} +\textcolor{blue}{\cancel{f^2}} &=\textcolor{green}{\cancel{X^2}}+\textcolor{red}{2Xf}+\textcolor{blue}{\cancel{f^2}}\nonumber \\
4A^2 -4A\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}&=4Xf \nonumber \\
A^2 - A\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2} &=fX \nonumber \\
A^2  - fX &=A\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}\nonumber \\
A  -\frac{ fX}{A} &=\sqrt{(X-f)^2+y^2}
\end{align}
Once again we square both sides to remove the square root
\begin{align}
\left(A  -\frac{ fX}{A}\right)^2 &=(X-f)^2+y^2\nonumber \\
A^2 - 2\textcolor{red}{\cancel{A}}\frac{fX}{\textcolor{red}{\cancel{A}}}+\left(\frac{f}{A}\right)^2 X^2 &=X^2 -2fX +f^2+y^2\nonumber \\
A^2 \textcolor{red}{\cancel{- 2fX}}+\left(\frac{f}{A}\right)^2 X^2 &=X^2 \textcolor{red}{\cancel{-2fX}} +f^2+y^2\nonumber \\
A^2-f^2 &=\left(1-\left(\frac{f}{A}\right)^2\right)X^2 +y^2\nonumber \\
&=  \frac{A^2-f^2}{A^2} X^2 + y^2\nonumber \\
1 &=  \frac{X^2}{A^2} + \frac{y^2}{A^2 -f^2}\nonumber 
\end{align}
This further simplifies if we use equation \refpage{eq:geom1} which with some rearrangement gives $B^2=A^2-f^2$.

And so we get the typical form of the equation of an ellipse
\begin{equation}
1=\frac{X^2}{A^2}+\frac{y^2}{B^2}\label{eq:geom-cart}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Equation for an ellipse in polar form}

Here we are going to look at the equation of an ellipse in polar coordinates with the positive focus at the origin. The closest point\footnote{Which as we will see later is called the periapsis for an orbit} lies along the positive x axis. This is the same system as we have been working with but shifted along the x axis so that the focus it at the origin, alternatively we can continue to use our centred ellipse but measure everything from the positive focus. This is in fact what we will do here and is also why we have made the distiction between the $X$ coordinate and the $x$ coordinate. Before looking at the relationship between $X$ and $x$, we will look at the polar angle for an ellipse\footnote{for orbits, this angle is called the true anomaly}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=0.3\textheight]{./img/string-method-polar.png} 
\caption{The polar angle for an ellipse} \label{fig:string-method-polar}
\end{figure}

As you can see from Figure \ref{fig:string-method-polar} we can determine the point $P$ based on the polar angle $\nu$

\begin{align}
X &= f + r \cos \nu \nonumber \\
y &= r \sin \nu \label{eq:geom-Xy}
\end{align}

we can also define $x$ now also

\begin{equation}
x = r \cos \nu \label{eq:geom-x}
\end{equation}

Going back to the sum equation\footnote{Equation \ref{eq:geom-sum} on page \pageref{eq:geom-sum}}

\begin{align}
S &= r + d \nonumber \\
2A - r &= d \nonumber \\
(2 A - r)^2 &= d^2 \nonumber \\
(2 A - r)^2 &= (X+f)^2 + y^2 \nonumber \\
4A^2-4Ar + r^2 &= X^2+2Xf + f^2 + y^2 \nonumber \\
4A^2-4Ar + r^2 &= X^2+2Xf + f^2 + y^2 \nonumber \\
 &= (f+r\cos \nu)^2 + 2f^2 + 2fr\cos \nu  +f^2 +y^2 \nonumber \\
 &=\textcolor{blue}{f^2}+\textcolor{green}{2fr\cos \nu} + r^2\cos^2 \nu + \textcolor{blue}{2f^2} +\textcolor{green}{ 2fr\cos \nu}  +\textcolor{blue}{f^2} +y^2 \nonumber \\
 &=4f^2+4fr\cos \nu + r^2\cos^2 \nu +y^2 \nonumber \\
 &=4f^2+4fr\cos \nu + r^2\cos^2 \nu +r^2 \sin^2 \nu \nonumber \\
 &=4f^2+4fr\cos \nu + r^2 \nonumber \\
4A^2-4Ar + \textcolor{red}{\cancel{r^2}}&= 4f^2+4fr\cos \nu + \textcolor{red}{\cancel{r^2}} \nonumber \\
4A^2-4Ar&= 4f^2+4fr\cos \nu \nonumber \\
A^2-Ar&= f^2+fr\cos \nu \nonumber \\
A^2-f^2&= Ar+fr\cos \nu \nonumber \\
A^2-f^2&=r(A+f\cos \nu) \nonumber \\
r &=\frac{A^2-f^2}{A+f\cos \nu}
\end{align}

We can do a little further simplifying by using Equation \refpage{eq:geom-eccentricity}

\begin{align}
r &=\frac{A^2-f^2}{A+f\cos \nu} \nonumber \\
&=\frac{A^2-e^2A^2}{A+eA\cos \nu} \nonumber \\
&=\frac{A^2(1-e^2)}{A(1+e\cos \nu)} \nonumber \\
&=\frac{A(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos \nu} \label{eq:geom-polar-form}
\end{align}

Which is the standard form.

\section{The Auxiliary Circle}

Another parameterisation for an ellipse comes from looking at the relationship to a circle. In other words, an ellipse can also be seen as a flattened circle.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=0.3\textheight]{./img/auxiliary-circle.png} 
\caption{The ellipse and the auxiliary circle} \label{fig:auxiliary-circle}
\end{figure}

Consider a circle with radius $A$ which is centred on the ellipse with a semi-major axis of length $A$ and a semi-minor axis of length $B$, as in Figure \ref{fig:auxiliary-circle}. The circle is known as the Auxiliary Circle. We can define a point $Q$ on the circle as $(A \cos E, A \sin E)$ so that the line from the origin to $Q$ has an angle $E$\footnote{This angle is called the eccentric anomaly in terms of orbits} with the x axis.

We can now choose point $P$ that is on the ellipse so that $Q$ and $P$ share the same x coordinate. We can work out the corresponding y coordinate by using the equation of an ellipse\footnote{Equation \ref{eq:geom-cart} on page \pageref{eq:geom-cart}}.

\begin{align}
\frac{X^2}{A^2}+\frac{y^2}{B^2}&=1 \nonumber \\
\frac{A^2\cos^2 E}{A^2}+\frac{y^2}{B^2}&=1 \nonumber \\
\cos^2 E+\frac{y^2}{B^2}&=1 \nonumber \\
\frac{y^2}{B^2}&=1 -\cos^2 E\nonumber \\
\frac{y^2}{B^2}&=\sin^2 E\nonumber \\
y^2&=B^2\sin^2 E\nonumber \\
y & = B \sin E 
\end{align}

Which leads to one of the standard parameterisations $P=(A \cos E, B \sin E)$

\section{The Apses}

The apses are the closest and furthest point from the positive focus of the ellipse.  The periapsis is the closest point and the apoapsis is the farthest point. We will lable these $p$ and $a$ respectively.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[height=0.5\textheight]{./img/geometry-apses.png} 
\caption{The ellipse with the apses highlighted} \label{fig:geometry-apses}
\end{figure}

As we can see from Figure \ref{fig:geometry-apses} that the periapsis has a length of $p=A-f$ and the apoapsis has a length of $a=A+f$. We can put these in terms of the semi-major axis and the eccentricity by using equation \refpage{eq:geom-eccentricity}

\begin{align}
p&=A(1-e) \nonumber \\
a&=A(1+e)
\end{align}

We can also see that $A=p+a$ and that $f=a-p$

\section{Relationship between true and eccentric anomalies}

Here we will show that there is a relationship between the angles $\nu$ in Figure \refpage{fig:string-method-polar} and $E$ in Figure \refpage{fig:auxiliary-circle}. In terms of orbits as we will see later, $\nu$ is called the true anomaly and $E$ is the eccentric anomaly.

We start by looking at the tangent of $\nu$ and using equation \refpage{eq:geom1} in particular the form $B=A\sqrt{1-e^2}$ and equation \refpage{eq:geom-eccentricity} in particular the form $f=eA$. Finally we need equations \refpage{eq:geom-Xy} and \refpage{eq:geom-x}.

\begin{align}
x &= r \cos \nu \nonumber \\
 &= A \cos E -f  \nonumber \\
&= A \cos E - eA  \nonumber \\
&= A(\cos E -e)
\end{align}

\begin{align}
y &= r \sin \nu \nonumber \\
 &= B \sin E \nonumber \\
&= A\sqrt{1-e^2} \sin E
\end{align}

We can now put these into

\begin{align}
\tan \nu &= \frac{y}{x} \nonumber \\
 &= \frac{A\sqrt{1-e^2} \sin E}{A(\cos E -e)} \nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \sin E}{\cos E -e}
\end{align}

Here we can use the half tan formulas \refpage{eq:trig1} and \refpage{eq:trig2} to replace the sine and cosine
\begin{align}
\tan \nu &= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \sin E}{\cos E -e} \nonumber \\
 &= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \frac{2 \tan \frac{E}{2}}{1+\tan^2 \frac{E}{2}}}{\frac{1- \tan^2 \frac{E}{2}}{1+\tan^2 \frac{E}{2}} -e}
\end{align}
Because the next steps can get a little messy, we'll introduce some variables to help, let $t = \tan  \frac{E}{2}$

\begin{align}
\tan \nu&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \frac{2 \tan \frac{E}{2}}{1+\tan^2 \frac{E}{2}}}{\frac{1- \tan^2 \frac{E}{2}}{1+\tan^2 \frac{E}{2}} -e}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \frac{2 t}{1+t^2}}{\frac{1- t^2}{1+t^2} -e}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} \frac{2 t}{1+t^2}}{\frac{1- t^2-e -e t^2}{1+t^2}}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} 2 t}{1- t^2-e -e t^2}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} 2 t}{1-e-(1+e)t^2}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{1-e^2} 2 t}{(1+e)\left(\frac{1-e}{1+e}-t^2\right)}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{\sqrt{(1-e)(1+e)} 2 t}{(1+e)\left(\frac{1-e}{1+e}-t^2\right)}\nonumber \\
&= \sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\frac{2 t}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\right)^2-t^2}
\end{align}
Again for ease, let $\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}$
\begin{align}
\tan \nu&= \sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\frac{2 t}{\left(\sqrt{\frac{1-e}{1+e}}\right)^2-t^2} \nonumber \\
&= \alpha \frac{2 t}{\alpha^2-t^2}\nonumber \\
&= \alpha \frac{2 t}{\alpha^2 \left(1-\frac{t^2}{\alpha^2}\right)}\nonumber \\
&= \frac{2\frac{t}{\alpha}}{ \left(1-\frac{t^2}{\alpha^2}\right)}\nonumber \\
\end{align}
Now we now use Equation \refpage{eq:trig3} to replace $\tan \nu$ with $\tan \frac{\nu}{2}$
\begin{align}
\tan \nu&= \frac{2\frac{t}{\alpha}}{ \left(1-\frac{t^2}{\alpha^2}\right)}\nonumber \\
\frac{2 \tan \frac{\nu}{2}}{1-\tan^2 \frac{\nu}{2}} &= \frac{2\frac{t}{\alpha}}{ \left(1-\frac{t^2}{\alpha^2}\right)}\nonumber \\
\end{align}
you can see that
\begin{equation}
\tan \frac{\nu}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1+e}{1-e}}\tan \frac{E}{2} \label{eq:geom2}
\end{equation}
